## 1. What the project is

**The problem.** Translate American Sign Language video into English sentences, running entirely on a
small embedded device rather than in the cloud, within a 15-watt power budget.

**Why that is hard.** Sign language translation needs a pose-estimation network (to find hands, face
and body in each frame) and a language model (to turn a sequence of poses into a sentence). Both are
expensive. Published work runs them on desktop GPUs drawing 250–400 W. We have 15 W, which is roughly
20× less, and 8 GB of memory shared with the operating system.

**The hardware.** One NVIDIA Jetson Orin Nano 8 GB developer kit, called `jetson-lpcv-03` on the lab
network. Linux for Tegra R36.4.7, JetPack 6.2.1. It is shared, course-owned equipment: **we have no
root access, ever**, and must not leave large files on it. Power is fixed at the 15 W profile.

**The pipeline**, four stages:

```
ASL video  →  [1] crop to signer  →  [2] pose network  →  [3] Uni-Sign ST-GCN  →  [4] mT5-base  →  English
                                     133 keypoints        pose encoder            text decoder
                                     per frame            (frozen)                (frozen)
```

- **[2] Pose network.** Per frame, outputs 133 "whole-body" keypoints — 17 body, 6 feet, 68 face,
  21 left hand, 21 right hand — each an (x, y) pixel location plus a confidence score. We evaluate two
  candidate networks (see §2).
- **[3] Uni-Sign ST-GCN.** A spatio-temporal graph convolutional network that consumes the keypoint
  sequence and produces embeddings for the language model. It uses only **69 of the 133** keypoints,
  split into four groups (body, left hand, right hand, face). We use the authors' released weights
  **frozen — we do not train anything.** This matters constantly: it means our pose output must look
  like the pose data this network was trained on, or it degrades.
- **[4] mT5-base.** A multilingual text-to-text transformer that decodes the embeddings into an English
  sentence. Also frozen.

**The deliverable.** A study measuring the accuracy/energy trade-off of compressing this pipeline, with
every hardware number from a real on-device run. The **week-4 gate** requires "M1 plus one compressed
row on each side with measured energy" (see §2 for M1). If we miss it, the fallback is to cut the LLM
post-correction stage and the phone app, and ship a Jetson-only study.

**The team.** Two people. **Tushar** owns the pose front-end and *every* board run. **Atisri** owns the
language-model track. This briefing is written from Tushar's side, so pose detail is deeper than LM
detail.

**The dataset: OpenASL.** 976 test clips of ASL with English sentence translations. It is
"distribution-by-reference": the dataset ships **no video**, only YouTube IDs, time ranges, signer
bounding boxes and sentences. We download clips ourselves. The authors separately released their own
pre-extracted pose files, which we use as a reference (see §2, "ceiling row").

### Technical terms

- **FP32 / FP16 / INT8.** Numeric precision: 32-bit float, 16-bit float, 8-bit integer. Lower
  precision is faster and cheaper but can lose accuracy or overflow. FP16's largest representable
  value is **65504**.
- **Mixed precision.** Running most of a network in FP16 but pinning specific layers to FP32 — our fix
  for RTMPose-x's overflow (7 layers out of 465).
- **TensorRT.** NVIDIA's compiler that turns a neural network into an optimised "engine" for a
  specific GPU. Engines are built on the device and are not portable.
- **TF32.** A reduced-precision mode Ampere GPUs use *by default* for FP32 matrix multiplies. An
  "FP32" engine built with defaults is therefore not really FP32 — this bit us (§7).
- **simcc.** The output representation both pose networks use. Instead of predicting a coordinate
  directly, the network predicts a probability distribution over **discrete bins** along the x and y
  axes, and the coordinate is the argmax (the highest-scoring bin). Two consequences that recur
  throughout this document:
  1. **All coordinate errors are whole multiples of one bin.** There is no such thing as a 0.5-bin
     error. Sub-bin numbers in a table are an averaging artefact.
  2. **An "argmax flip"** is when a nearly-flat distribution — typical of an occluded or
     low-confidence joint — has its peak move to a neighbouring or distant bin under a tiny numerical
     perturbation. This produces a huge *pixel* error from a *negligible* numerical change. It is a
     discrete decision changing on a near-tie, not a precision failure.
- **Bin width in pixels** is not a constant. We derived it as
  `max(crop_w, crop_h × 0.75) × 1.25 / (input_w × simcc_split_ratio)` — so it **scales with how large
  the signer is in frame**. Measured: 1.71–2.45 px for RTMW, 1.14–1.63 px for RTMPose-x across our five
  clips. This is why pixel thresholds are treacherous (§7).
- **Confidence threshold 0.3.** `common/pose_to_unisign.py` zeroes any keypoint scoring below 0.3
  before the ST-GCN sees it. So a low-confidence joint's *position* cannot affect the translation at
  all. We therefore only judge keypoints the FP32 reference scores ≥ 0.3.
- **"Threshold flip" / "score-threshold crossing".** A keypoint that is above 0.3 in one engine and
  below it in the other. This changes the ST-GCN's input even at zero pixels of movement, because the
  joint is present in one and zeroed in the other.
- **BLEU / ROUGE-L.** Standard translation metrics, 0–100, higher is better. BLEU-4 rewards matching
  4-word sequences; it is harsh and noisy on short texts. ROUGE-L is recall-oriented on the longest
  common subsequence and is somewhat steadier.
- **The "ceiling row".** The OpenASL authors' *own* released pose files, run through *our* evaluation
  path. Because the ST-GCN and mT5 are frozen and identical in both cases, comparing our poses to
  theirs isolates the cost of **our pose front-end** from the cost of the model. It is an upper bound
  on what our pose stage could achieve.
- **nvpmodel.** Jetson's power-profile selector. Mode 0 = 15 W (GPU capped at 612 MHz), mode 1 = 25 W
  (918 MHz). **We use mode 0 only.** Reading the mode needs no root; changing it does.
- **nvmap.** Jetson's GPU memory allocator. It allocates only from genuinely free memory and **will not
  reclaim page cache**, so the board reports CUDA out-of-memory while `free` shows 3–4 GB cached. Our
  workaround is `jetson/drop_file_cache.py`, which uses `posix_fadvise(DONTNEED)` and needs no root.
- **Energy per frame (mJ/frame).** Our primary efficiency metric, *not* latency. Reason in §5.

## 3. Everything measured

All board numbers: `jetson-lpcv-03`, nvpmodel mode 0 (15 W), clocks sampled during the run.

### 3.1 Pose front-end speed and energy

Column definitions, because several of these are easy to misread:
- **TRT ms** — GPU inference only, but measured CPU-side around a stream synchronise, so it also
  includes the host→device upload of the input tensor (590 KB), not just kernel time.
- **total ms/frame** = preprocess + TRT + postprocess. **fps (compute)** = 1000 / total ms.
- **fps (end-to-end)** is a *different, lower* number: the harness's wall clock also pays JPEG decode
  from disk and result serialisation, which sit outside `total_ms`. At 15 W that gap is 5.5–7.0 ms per
  frame, so the two fps figures differ by 20–25 %. §3.1b measures the split. **Never quote one of
  these next to the other's latency**: 19.7 ms/frame and 40 fps are both correct and are not the
  same measurement (19.7 ms → 51 fps compute; 40 fps → 25.2 ms wall).
- **mJ/frame** — *total* energy per frame from the board's VDD_IN rail, **including the ~3.6 W idle
  draw**. **dyn mJ/frame** — *dynamic* energy, i.e. above idle. **These two give different speedup
  factors and both are legitimate; always say which one you mean.** Total is what a battery pays;
  dynamic is what the optimisation actually changed.
- **GPU MHz** is the mean over *busy* samples, not the cap. It varies per config because the governor
  downclocks on lighter work — this is why energy, not latency, is our cross-precision metric (§4.5).

| Config | GPU MHz (busy mean) | CPU MHz | TRT ms | total ms/frame | mJ/frame *(total)* | dyn mJ/frame *(above idle)* | avg W | fps *(compute)* | fps *(end-to-end)* |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| RTMPose-x 384×288 FP32 — *original baseline* | 612 | 866 | 66.7 | 79.2 | 733 | 410 | 8.20 | 12.6 | 11.5 |
| RTMW-l-m 256×192 FP32 | 598 | 904 | 25.6 | 33.0 | 275 | 123 | 6.53 | 30.3 | 25.0 |
| **RTMW-l-m 256×192 FP16** — *provisional choice* | **310** | **1029** | **13.8** | **19.7** | **127** | **34** | **4.93** | **50.8** | **39.9** |
| RTMPose-x FP16 plain | 414 | — | 22.7 | 33.5 | 254 | 106 | 6.20 | *broken, see §5* | *broken* |
| RTMPose-x FP16 mixed (7/465 layers FP32) | 413 | 919 | 23.3 | 35.0 | 264 | 110 | 6.15 | 28.6 | 23.9 |

The FP16 row previously read **40** in the compute-fps column. That was wrong — it was the
end-to-end figure from the harness, while every other row held 1000/total ms. Both columns are now
shown for every row and both come from the run JSONs (`fps_compute`, `fps_end_to_end`).

All rows: `jetson-lpcv-03`, nvpmodel mode 0, 3×299 frames of one clip, Tj 50–53 °C, idle 3.59–3.62 W.

**Net gain: 4.0× the frame rate on the compute basis (3.5× end-to-end), and either 5.8× or 12× less energy per frame depending on which energy
you quote** — 5.8× on total mJ/frame (733 → 127), 12× on dynamic mJ/frame (410 → 34). The gap between
those two factors is the fixed idle draw, which shrinks as a fraction of nothing: at 19.7 ms/frame the
board's 3.6 W baseline accounts for 71 mJ of the 127. **A report should quote the total figure as the
headline and the dynamic figure as the engine-level result**, because a reader who sees 12× without the
qualifier will assume total-system energy dropped that much, and it did not.

A 10-second sentence costs 38 J of pose extraction instead of 219 J (total basis). The pose stage now
clears the 30 fps source rate on either fps basis — 51 fps of compute, 40 fps including the harness's
JPEG decode — where the original baseline (12.6 / 11.5) cleared it on neither.

### 3.1b What the frame time is made of, and the CPU/GPU power coupling

`total_ms` above excludes two real costs, which is why the two fps columns differ. Both RTMW engines,
mode 0, 3×299 frames, board verified idle first (`jetson/run.sh whoelse`):

| stage | in `total_ms`? | FP16 | FP32 | what it is |
|---|---|---:|---:|---|
| `imread_ms` | **no** | 5.33 | 6.83 | JPEG decode from disk (`cv2.imread`) |
| `preprocess_ms` | yes | 4.76 | 6.10 | affine warp, BGR→RGB, normalise, transpose — CPU numpy |
| `trt_ms` | yes | 13.66 | 25.83 | input upload + `execute_async_v3` + stream sync |
| `postprocess_ms` | yes | 1.28 | 1.22 | simcc D2H copy (477 KB), argmax, affine back |
| **`total_ms`** | — | **19.71** | **33.15** | the pose stage |
| `collect_ms` | **no** | 0.16 | 0.16 | keypoints → Python lists |
| residual | **no** | 0.01 | 0.01 | loop overhead; the accounting closes |
| **wall ms/frame** | — | **25.20** | **40.14** | |

So the 19.7 → 25.2 ms difference is **JPEG decode plus 0.16 ms of serialisation, and nothing else**.
Prefer **fps (compute)** when reasoning about the design — a deployed pipeline decodes H.264 on NVDEC,
so the 5.3 ms is an artifact of reading pre-extracted JPEGs — and **fps (end-to-end)** when describing
what this harness does.

**The CPU stages are not constants.** `imread` and `preprocess` both slow by **×1.281** (identical to
three digits, on identical inputs) from the FP16 run to the FP32 run, while measured `cpu0_MHz` falls
1045 → 897 (×1.165); DRAM contention with a 604 MHz GPU covers the rest. Under a 15 W cap a hungrier
engine starves the CPU. Two consequences worth stating in a report: comparing `pre ms` across engines
partly measures the governor, and **2.8 of the 14.9 ms/frame that FP16 saves end-to-end is the CPU
stages speeding up**, not the engine (which accounts for 12.2 ms).

Raw: `results/p2b_split_rtmw_{fp16,fp32}.json`.

**One row you may see elsewhere and should not compare against these:** an earlier FP32 RTMPose-x
measurement of 44.4 ms TRT / 56.4 ms total / 754 mJ, taken on the *other* board. Its clock was never
logged and is inferred to be ~918 MHz, i.e. the 25 W cap rather than our 15 W cap. Latency is not
comparable across power modes; energy nearly is (754 vs 733 mJ).

### 3.2 Keypoint agreement — does lower precision move the keypoints?

Method (`task1_rtmpose/07_kpt_agreement.py`): run the FP16 engine and its *own* FP32 engine over the
same frames and measure the Euclidean pixel distance per keypoint, judged only where FP32 scores ≥ 0.3.
This measures **precision damage in isolation** — same network, same input, only the arithmetic differs.

**Five signers, 1510 frames, ~104k confident keypoints per config:**

| config | Uni-Sign-consumed ≤5 px | face | hands | body | threshold flips |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| RTMW FP16 | **99.81 %** *(n=104,113)* | 100.00 % *(n=102,680)* | 99.69 % *(n=63,343)* | 99.87 % *(n=19,589)* | 33 of 200,830 (0.016 %) |
| RTMPose-x FP16 mixed | **99.75 %** *(n=103,301)* | 100.00 % *(n=102,680)* | 99.61 % *(n=62,604)* | 99.76 % *(n=19,118)* | 27 of 200,830 (0.013 %) |

Each percentage's denominator `n` is the number of *confident* keypoints in that group (FP32 score
≥ 0.3), which is why the counts differ between configs and between groups. Threshold flips are counted
over **all** 1510 frames × 133 keypoints, confident or not, since a flip is by definition a change in
confidence.

**One signer, 299 frames** (the broken engine was excluded from the five-signer run as already
disqualified):

| config | Uni-Sign-consumed ≤5 px | face | median face error |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| RTMPose-x FP16 **plain** | 86.5 % | **49.6 %** | **108 px** |

Reading it: the two repaired/clean engines are essentially identical to their FP32 selves. The plain
FP16 RTMPose-x engine is genuinely destroyed — its *median* face keypoint is 108 px out.

**Caveat that matters for interpreting any pixel threshold:** the ≤1 px and ≤2 px columns we previously
reported are one simcc bin apart, and the bin scales with signer size. RTMW's ≤2 px figure reads
99.2–99.4 % on three clips and 86.5–87.9 % on two others — **a 13-point swing caused purely by how
large the signer is in frame, with no accuracy difference behind it.** Only the ≤5 px column compares
across clips and models, because 5 px is "within two bins" everywhere (largest bin 2.45 px).

**Hands are the accuracy floor in every configuration**, and the residual is *not* a precision effect:
hand agreement is the same in the broken and the repaired RTMPose-x engine (98.87 % vs 98.93 %). It is
argmax instability on low-confidence hand joints. The worst confident keypoint is a hand joint in both
good engines — 122.7 px at reference score 0.337 for RTMW, 229.1 px at 0.470 for mixed. **Hands carry
most of the sign lexicon, so this is the wrong group to have as the floor**, and nothing done so far
improves it.

### 3.3 Pose → translation quality (the end-to-end accuracy test)

Five clips, each pose config → the frozen ST-GCN + mT5 on the board. The **ceiling row** is the
authors' own released poses through the identical path.

| poses | BLEU-1 | BLEU-4 | ROUGE-L | preds identical to ceiling | to own FP32 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| **Authors' released poses (ceiling)** | 57.30 | **16.23** | **46.17** | 5/5 | — |
| RTMW-l-m FP32 | 47.68 | 9.89 | 43.39 | 1/5 | — |
| **RTMW-l-m FP16** | 47.68 | 9.89 | 43.39 | 1/5 | **5/5** |
| RTMPose-x FP32 | 39.32 | 8.96 | 31.94 | 0/5 | — |
| RTMPose-x FP16 mixed | 42.62 | 9.39 | 33.76 | 0/5 | 3/5 |

**Do not read the BLEU column as an absolute score.** Five sentences cannot support a BLEU comparison:
the authors' own poses score 16.23 here against **22.53–22.67 on the full 976-clip split** with the same
frozen checkpoint and the same metric code. The subset alone moves BLEU-4 by 6 points. Differences below
~1 BLEU-4 in this table are noise.

**The reliable signal is the identical-prediction count**, which is exact and discrete:

- **RTMW FP16 produces 5/5 token-identical predictions to RTMW FP32.** FP16 costs *exactly nothing* in
  output text on this sample. This is a within-extractor comparison and is the most solid accuracy
  result we have.
- **RTMPose-x mixed precision is *not* neutral: 3/5.** Two of five sentences change. This matches the
  ordering §3.2 gave from keypoints alone (99.75 % vs RTMW's 99.81 %), which is the first evidence that
  the keypoint metric predicts downstream behaviour.

### 3.4 Which extractor produced the authors' poses — measured, not assumed

**This is the project's live controversy.** The Uni-Sign paper states it extracts its 133 keypoints
with **RTMPose-x**, and our four keypoint index groups come from that paper verbatim. But the OpenASL
pose archive the frozen checkpoint actually consumes measures as **192-wide**, i.e. RTMW's resolution,
not RTMPose-x's.

Method: a simcc network quantises coordinates to bins, so the smallest gap between coordinates within
one frame *is* one bin. The ratio `bbox span / bin` is scale-free and equals
`input_w × split_ratio / padding`, so it identifies the model's input width.

| poses | span / bin | vs ours-192 | vs ours-288 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| ours, RTMW-l-m 256×192 | 291 | 1.000 | — |
| ours, RTMPose-x 384×288 | 447 | 1.539 *(theory 1.500 ✓)* | 1.000 |
| **authors' released OpenASL poses** | **293** | **1.008** | 0.655 |

The estimator is validated by our own two engines recovering 288/192 = 1.500 to within 2.6 %. The
authors' poses match a 192-wide extractor to **0.8 %** and miss a 288-wide one by **35 %**. This agrees
with their archive folder being named `pose-rtmpose-192`.

**Honest caveat:** the ratio conflates input width with `simcc_split_ratio` and bbox padding, so a
288-wide model padded ~1.9× would fit the same number. 192 is the best-supported reading, **not a
proof.** The likeliest reconciliation is that the paper describes their pipeline (notably for CSL-News,
their own dataset) while the distributed OpenASL poses were produced at 192. Since **we use the
checkpoint frozen**, what governs us is the archive's distribution rather than the paper's prose — but
anyone *training* a model should follow the paper and use RTMPose-x.

**We ruled out the obvious competing explanation, that our RTMPose-x is simply broken.** Its FP32
engine matched its PyTorch checkpoint to **8e-6**, and its per-group confidences track RTMW's closely
(body 0.627 vs 0.715 mean; face 100 % vs 100 % above threshold; hands 98.4/99.1 % vs 99.9/99.9 %). The
one striking difference — 0.3 % vs 16.9 % of *foot* keypoints above threshold — is both models failing
on feet, with RTMPose-x landing just under the 0.3 gate and RTMW just over it (means 0.136 vs 0.208).
Feet are not among the 69 keypoints Uni-Sign consumes. So RTMPose-x extracts correctly and is simply
off-distribution for this frozen checkpoint.

### 3.4b The crop experiment — a hypothesis tested and dropped

§3.3 left a 6.3 BLEU-4 gap to the ceiling and blamed our crop convention. **Tested, and the crop is not
the cause.** We re-fetched the same five clips with OpenASL's own recipe (square the bbox, black-pad
outside the frame, resize to 224) and re-ran all four engines. The geometry was validated against the
authors' released keypoints first, so this is their crop rather than an approximation of it.

Their frames turn out to be **15–50 % black padding**, because the OpenASL bboxes extend far outside the
frame. Our confident keypoints duly moved from spanning ~0.01–0.99 of the frame to ~0.14–0.88, against the
authors' ~0.10–0.91 — so the intended distribution shift did happen.

| poses | BLEU-4 | ROUGE-L | = ceiling | = own FP32 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| ceiling (authors' poses) | 16.23 | 46.17 | 5/5 | — |
| RTMW FP32, native crop | 9.89 | **43.39** | 1/5 | — |
| RTMW FP32, openasl crop | **12.38** | 38.87 | 1/5 | — |
| RTMW FP16, native crop | 9.89 | 43.39 | 1/5 | **5/5** |
| RTMW FP16, openasl crop | 8.64 | 36.77 | 0/5 | **3/5** |
| RTMPose-x FP32, native / openasl | 8.96 / 5.95 | 31.94 / 30.37 | 0/5 | — |
| RTMPose-x FP16 mixed, native / openasl | 9.39 / 6.21 | 33.76 / 30.22 | 0/5 | 3/5 / 3/5 |

- **Not supported.** BLEU-4 rose while ROUGE-L fell — the two metrics disagree in direction — and the one
  discrete measure, predictions matching the ceiling, stayed at 1/5. RTMPose-x got worse on both metrics.
- **Extractor identity is now the leading explanation.** Their 224 crop *discards* resolution (our native
  crops are 502–754 px wide), so our pose input is better-resolved and theirs still translates better.
  That rules out crop convention and pose sharpness together, leaving their network's keypoint placement
  conventions, which the frozen ST-GCN learned. Not fixable by re-cropping.
- **A result of ours is retracted here.** "RTMW FP16 is translation-neutral (5/5 identical)" gives **3/5**
  on this crop. Measured in bins — the only cross-crop-valid unit — keypoint agreement is *unchanged*
  (≤2 bins: 99.39 % native, 99.76 % openasl), so the jump is the fragility of a five-sentence sample, not
  a real effect. **The robust claim is the keypoint agreement; sentence-level neutrality is unproven at
  n=5** and must be re-tested on ~25 clips before any report repeats it.
- **One ordering reproduces across both crops**, which is stronger than §3.3's single-crop version: RTMW
  agrees with its own FP32 better than RTMPose-x mixed does (≤2 bins: 99.39 % vs 96.58 % native, 99.76 %
  vs 98.72 % openasl; worst 53 vs 147 bins native, 44 vs 77 openasl).
- **Sharpest illustration of the bin problem.** The same engine pair reads 99.81 % within 5 px at native
  crop and 99.99 % at 224, implying the small crop is numerically safer. It is not: 5 px is ~2 bins at
  native and ~7 bins at 224. In bins the two are within 0.4 pp.

|---|---|---|---:|---:|---:|
| L4 PyTorch, full | full 582M | PyTorch FP32 | 69 | 2.41 GB | not measured |
| L4 PyTorch, pruned | **pruned 238M** | PyTorch FP32 | 55 | 1.03 GB | not measured |
| L8.2 TensorRT | **pruned 238M** | TRT FP32 | **19.2** | not measured | **2.07 GB** |

- **The TensorRT engines were built from the pruned checkpoint**, so "pruned + TRT FP32" *is* a measured
  combination (`models/mt5_pruned_onnx`, decoded with `openasl_pose_only_slt_pruned.pth`). The **2.9×
  speedup is pruned-vs-pruned on the same board**: 19.2 ms/token against the *pruned* PyTorch row's
  55 ms/token, not against the full model's 69.
- **Why 2.07 GB exceeds the pruned PyTorch 1.03 GB, despite being the same weights.** Two separate
  reasons, both easy to misread as a contradiction:
  1. **The metrics differ.** 1.03 GB is peak GPU memory *allocated by the torch allocator*. 2.07 GB is
     *MemFree consumed by the whole process* — engine weights + activations + TRT execution context +
     CUDA context. They are not the same measurement and neither bounds the other.
  2. **The engine set duplicates the decoder weights.** Engine sizes are encoder 341 MB + decoder_init
     616 MB + decoder_step 558 MB = **1.515 GB**, against a 571 MB pruned checkpoint. The decoder appears
     *twice* because the explicit-KV-cache export splits it into two separate graphs (`decoder_init` for
     the first pass, `decoder_step` for subsequent tokens) and each carries its own copy of the weights.
- **So the real trade-off is: TensorRT buys 2.9× decoder speed at roughly 2× the memory**, and the memory
  cost is an artefact of the three-graph export rather than of TensorRT itself. Sharing weights between
  the two decoder graphs, or folding them into one engine with a dynamic branch, is the obvious lever and
  has not been attempted.
- **Reduced precision buys nothing on the decoder and breaks correctness.** FP16 engines are numerically
  broken for mT5 (every step returns token 0 with logprob −10.169 = −ln(26078), a uniform distribution
  from saturated logits — the known T5/mT5 FP16 overflow). BF16 runs and is coherent but diverges from the
  FP32 greedy path at step 3. Crucially the FP16 decoder step is *no faster* (18 vs 19 ms/token), because
  the step is host-bound, not GEMM-bound. **The deployable LM engine set is therefore FP32.**
- One counter-intuitive detail worth not losing: **the TRT encoder is *slower* than PyTorch** (82 vs
  57 ms). With TF32 off, TensorRT falls back to plain FP32 FFMA kernels while PyTorch's cuBLAS has better
  FP32 GEMM tiles. It costs ~25 ms per sentence against ~870 ms saved on the decoder, so it is worth it,
  but "TensorRT is faster" is false for the encoder.

## 4. Decisions taken, and the reason for each

1. **RTMW-l-m FP16 is the pose front-end — provisionally, and mostly on cost.** 1.8× faster and 2×
   cheaper per frame than RTMPose-x FP16 mixed, so it wins even at equal accuracy. The accuracy
   argument (§3.3) only needs to survive "not much worse". **See §6 — this is the weakest major
   decision in the project.**
2. **FP16 is adopted for the pose stage.** 5/5 identical predictions vs its own FP32, plus 99.81 %
   keypoint agreement. This half is solid and independent of decision 1.
3. **The language model stays FP32 on the board.** FP16 mT5 produces uniform logits (overflow); BF16
   has the range but its 8-bit mantissa breaks token-exact greedy decoding at step 3. Reduced precision
   also bought no speed, because the decoder is launch-bound, not compute-bound.
4. **TF32 is explicitly cleared for every FP32 baseline.** TensorRT enables it by default on Ampere, so
   a default-built "FP32" engine is not FP32: it failed the correctness check at 1.55e-3 and passed at
   7.2e-6 once cleared. **Any FP32 reference taken before this is invalid.**
5. **Energy per frame, not latency, is the cross-precision comparison.** The GPU governor downclocks
   when work is lighter — busy-sample mean 612 MHz for RTMPose-x FP32 but 310 MHz for RTMW FP16. A
   lighter engine therefore runs at a lower clock, and latency ratios *understate* the real gain.
6. **The max-pixel gate is demoted; the agreement distribution is the accuracy axis.** Full reasoning
   in §7.
7. **The gate threshold was not loosened to match.** Raising it from 2 px to 7 px would have converted
   three failures into passes without new evidence. Loosening a criterion to accommodate results you
   already have is how a project ratifies its own regression.
8. **Our crop deliberately differs from OpenASL's own `prep/crop_video.py`.** They square, black-pad
   and resize to 224; we scale the released bbox to the frame and clamp, keeping native resolution,
   because the pose models apply their own affine transform and padding changes what they see.
   **This is now a suspected confound — see §8 item 1.**
9. **Vocabulary pruning is accepted**: −59 % parameters and −52 % checkpoint size for a cost bounded at
   ~0.6 BLEU-4, with no retraining.
10. **Board hygiene is fixed policy**: never sudo; 15 W only; clocks logged never pinned; one engine per
    process; `--workspace-gb 1`; `jetson/drop_file_cache.py` before loading *any* large model; and
    results pulled only with `jetson/pull_results.sh` (a bare rsync silently reverts our own documents,
    which has happened).

## 5. Problems encountered, and the finding worth keeping from each

**TF32 masquerading as FP32.** Decision 4. The kind of bug that silently invalidates a baseline instead
of crashing.

**RTMPose-x FP16 was catastrophically broken — 383 px worst error, 40 % of keypoints usable.** Root
cause found by writing `08_fp16_range_scan.py`, which exposes every intermediate tensor of the ONNX
graph and compares its peak against FP16's 65504 ceiling: the head's ScaleNorm `ReduceSum` peaks at
**142,293**, 2.2× over the limit. **One tensor out of 376 saturates and takes the entire head down.**
The same scan over RTMW's 398 tensors finds *zero* overflowing tensors, which is exactly why its FP16
engine is clean. Fixed by pinning the seven `mlp.0` layers to FP32, costing under 3 % latency.

**The max-pixel gate could not distinguish a rounding artefact from a catastrophe.** Our original
correctness test gated on the single worst per-keypoint pixel error over 20 frames. Three failures
followed:
- It **worsens as you measure more frames** — RTMW FP16's worst went from 6.50 px over 20 frames to
  134 px over 299, because you sample more rare argmax flips.
- Its 2 px threshold sat *below* RTMW's own 2.17 px error quantum, so the engine could not pass unless
  bit-exact.
- All three FP16 engines returned the same verdict, covering **two deployable engines and one genuine
  catastrophe** with no way to tell them apart.

Replaced by the agreement distribution (§3.2), which separates them cleanly. The max gate is kept only
as a regression tripwire for FP32 builds and NaN detection, where a maximum is the right instrument.
**This is a genuine methodology finding for the report:** a worst-case criterion is the wrong instrument
for a network whose output is a discrete argmax.

**Pixel thresholds are not portable across clips.** §3.2. The bin scales with signer size, so any
threshold must be stated in bins, not pixels.

**8 GB board, no root, and nvmap will not reclaim page cache.** Engine builds *and* model loads fail
with out-of-memory while `free` shows 3–4 GB cached. `jetson/drop_file_cache.py` fixes it without
privileges. Originally treated as a build-time-only step, which cost a wasted round of five evaluation
runs; **the rule is now general — drop the cache before loading any large model.**

**Two boards disagreed by 50 % on the same engine** (66.7 ms vs 44.4 ms, 8.2 W vs 11.6 W). Our GPU was
pinned at 612 MHz whenever busy — the 15 W cap. Scaling by 612/918 reproduces the other number to
0.1 ms, so the earlier row was taken at the 25 W cap. `/var/lib/nvpmodel/status` can be stale, so clocks
are now sampled during the run rather than trusted from a file. **Latency rows are not comparable across
power modes; energy rows nearly are.**

**OpenASL ships no video**, and undocumented besides: label files are gzipped pickles, and bounding
boxes can fall outside [0, 1]. `data/openasl_fetch.py` handles all of it.

**yt-dlp returned HTTP 403 on every section download.** The installed build hands ffmpeg a URL bound to
the player client yt-dlp itself used, and ffmpeg's own request is rejected. Compounded because the
system `python3` is 3.9, which pins yt-dlp to an old release; a venv on Python 3.13 gets a working
build, with no cookies or sign-in needed.

**ffmpeg silently rounded our crops.** The `crop` filter rounds width and height *down* to even for
yuv420p chroma subsampling, so five clips with an odd dimension delivered JPEGs 1 px smaller than the
metadata recorded — and `pose_to_unisign` normalises keypoints by exactly those numbers. ~0.1 % error,
nothing would have flagged it.

**WireGuard and Claude access were mutually exclusive.** The lab tunnel is a full tunnel
(`AllowedIPs = 0.0.0.0/0`), so raising it moved the default route into the lab and killed the assistant
session's own connection. Sockets opened beforehand survive because macOS caches the route on the
socket — which is why the two appeared compatible when started in the right order. **Solved** with a
second tunnel `lab-split` using `AllowedIPs = 192.168.1.0/24` and no `DNS =` line.

### Process failures, recorded because they cost real time

- **`results/RESULTS.md` was clobbered** by a bare `rsync board:results/ results/`. The board carries a
  full stale copy of the repo, so the pull silently reverted the write-up; recovered by replaying edits
  from the session transcript. Prevented permanently by `jetson/pull_results.sh`, which excludes `*.md`.
- **A wrong engine size was transcribed** into the results table (96.5 MB for 68.7 MB), caught later
  against the build log. Hand-copying numbers from logs into markdown is the weakest link in the
  write-up process and still has no automated check.
- **A guard was written with the wrong bound** and rejected all 20 valid clip conversions: it capped
  normalised keypoints at 1.05, but RTMPose decodes over a bbox padded 1.25× beyond the crop, so edge
  joints legitimately reach ~1.125 — and the authors' own released files span −0.031 to 1.218.
  Out-of-frame coordinates are part of the format.
- **An extractor conclusion was overstated.** "RTMW is what the checkpoint was trained on" was written
  as established fact when it was an inference from a folder name, and it contradicted the paper. Caught
  by Tushar; now corrected and hedged throughout (§3.4, §6).

## 6. Certainty ledger — how much to trust each claim

**This section exists because §3 mixes claims of very different strength.** An LLM summarising this
project should carry these qualifiers.

| Claim | Confidence | Basis / limit |
|---|---|---|
| RTMW FP16 keypoints ≈ RTMW FP32 (99.4–99.8 % within 2 simcc bins) | **High** | Reproduced on two independent crops of the same clips |
| ~~RTMW FP16 ≡ RTMW FP32 in output text (5/5 identical)~~ **RETRACTED** | **Low** | The same engine pair gives 3/5 on a 224 crop of the same clips while keypoint agreement is unchanged in bins. The 5/5 was a 5-sample coin flip; sentence-level neutrality is unproven (§3.4b) |
| RTMW is 1.8× faster / 2× cheaper than RTMPose-x mixed | **High** | Direct board measurement, 3×299 frames, clocks logged |
| Plain FP16 RTMPose-x is unusable | **High** | 49.6 % face agreement, 108 px median; root cause identified at the tensor level |
| Energy/frame is the right cross-precision metric | **High** | Governor behaviour measured directly (612 vs 310 MHz) |
| The max-pixel gate is the wrong instrument | **High** | Three independent failure modes demonstrated |
| Bin width scales with signer size | **High** | Formula matches measurement to two decimals on 5 clips × 2 models |
| The authors' released poses are 192-wide | **Medium-high** | 0.8 % match vs 35 % miss, estimator validated — but conflates width with padding and split ratio; corroborated only by a folder name |
| Our RTMPose-x pipeline is correct (not buggy) | **Medium-high** | 8e-6 vs PyTorch; confidences track RTMW's |
| **RTMW is more *accurate* than RTMPose-x end-to-end** | **Low** | **5 sentences. 1/5 vs 0/5 ceiling matches. Contradicts the paper's stated extractor. Confounded by an untested crop mismatch.** |
| ~~The ceiling gap is caused by our crop~~ **TESTED, NOT SUPPORTED** | **High** (that it is *not* the crop) | Matching their recipe left ceiling-identical predictions at 1/5, raised BLEU-4 but lowered ROUGE-L (§3.4b) |
| The ceiling gap comes from their extractor's keypoint placement conventions | **Speculative, now the leading hypothesis** | Crop is ruled out, and their 224 crop has *less* resolution than ours, so it is not pose sharpness either |
| RTMW agrees with its own FP32 better than RTMPose-x mixed does | **Medium-high** | Same ordering on two independent crops, measured in bins |
| Hand error is argmax instability, not precision | **Medium-high** | Identical in broken and repaired engines; maxima grow with sample size as a rare-event tail should |
| Any BLEU number in §3.3 as an absolute score | **Do not use** | Same inputs score 16.23 on 5 clips vs 22.5 on 976 |

### Number pairs that look comparable and are not

These are the traps. Each one has already caused a misreading, or is one step away from it. **If you are
summarising this project, do not put any of these pairs in the same sentence without the qualifier.**

| Looks like | Actually | Why |
|---|---|---|
| pruned PyTorch **1.03 GB** vs TRT **2.07 GB** → "TRT wasn't pruned" | Both are the pruned model | Different metrics (torch peak-allocated vs whole-process MemFree), *and* the 3-graph export duplicates decoder weights (§3.5) |
| **5.8×** vs **12×** energy reduction | Both correct | Total mJ/frame vs dynamic mJ/frame (above the 3.6 W idle floor) |
| TRT **19 ms/token** vs PyTorch **69 ms/token** → "3.6×" | The speedup is **2.9×** | 19 must be compared against the *pruned* PyTorch row (55), not the full model's 69 |
| "TensorRT is faster" | True for the decoder, **false for the encoder** | TRT encoder 82 ms vs PyTorch 57 ms, because TF32-off falls back to FFMA kernels (§3.5) |
| RTMPose-x FP32 **44.4 ms** vs **66.7 ms** | Different boards *and* different power caps | The 44.4 row was ~918 MHz (25 W); ours is 612 MHz (15 W). Latency is not comparable across modes; energy nearly is |
| **19.7 ms/frame** and **40 fps** in one sentence | 19.7 ms → **51** fps; 40 fps → **25.2** ms | Two metrics: `total_ms` (pose stage) vs the harness wall clock, which also pays 5.3 ms of JPEG decode. This exact pair was wrong in this document until 2026-09-26 (§3.1b) |
| **pre ms** across engine configs | Not a property of the preprocessing code | CPU stages scale with the power the GPU leaves: ×1.281 slower at 897 MHz vs 1045 MHz CPU (§3.1b) |
| ≤1 px / ≤2 px agreement columns across clips | Not comparable | One simcc bin is 1.14–2.45 px depending on signer size; only ≤5 px is uniform (§3.2) |
| BLEU-4 **16.23** (ceiling, 5 clips) vs **22.5** (full split) | Same poses, same checkpoint | The 5-clip subset alone moves BLEU-4 by ~6 points. No BLEU number in §3.3 is an absolute score |
| keypoint agreement **99.8 %** vs BLEU gap to ceiling | Different questions | Agreement is engine-vs-its-own-FP32 (precision only); the ceiling gap is *our extractor* vs *theirs* |
| RTMW **99.81 %** vs RTMPose-x mixed **99.75 %** | Real but tiny ordering | Both are ~99.8 % *on a 5 px threshold*. The engine difference that survives is in **bins**: ≤2 bins 99.39 % vs 96.58 % (native crop) and 99.76 % vs 98.72 % (224 crop), worst case 53 vs 147 bins — the same ordering on two independent crops. **Do not use the old "5/5 vs 3/5 identical predictions" here; that was retracted (§3.4b)** |

**Known stale or absent:**
- **No end-to-end on-device run exists yet** (M1/C6). Every number is per-stage.
- **The C9 results protocol has never been applied retroactively.** It requires 3 runs mean ± std, ≥3
  clips, ≥3 signers and a 30-minute sustained thermal run. Existing latency rows are 3×299 frames of a
  *single* clip, and the sustained run has not been done at all. **This affects every row in §3.1.**
- **No thermal-throttling data.** All runs are short.
- **INT8 has not been attempted.**
- The Mac no longer has a working `mmpose`/torch environment, so **ONNX cannot currently be
  re-exported**; torch-dependent work runs on the board.

## 7. Open questions, in the order we intend to answer them

1. ~~Does our crop recipe cost us the gap to the ceiling?~~ **Answered 2026-09-26: no** (§3.4b). Matching
   their recipe did not move ceiling-identical predictions off 1/5. Since their crop also has *less*
   resolution than ours, neither crop nor pose sharpness explains the gap, and the live hypothesis is now
   their extractor's keypoint placement conventions — which a re-crop cannot fix and which would need
   their network or fine-tuning to test.
2. **RTMW or RTMPose-x?** Currently decided on cost with only 5 sentences of accuracy evidence, against
   a paper that specifies RTMPose-x. Settling it needs ~25 more clips: the authors' poses for all 976
   test clips are already downloaded, so only the videos need fetching, plus one board pass repeating
   §3.3. **Until then the front-end choice should be described as provisional in any report.**
3. **M1 / C6: the first on-device end-to-end translation.** The Stage-0 baseline everything is compared
   against, and the week-4 gate depends on it. It can proceed on RTMW FP16 regardless of question 2,
   since a later extractor switch changes one engine path and not the harness.
4. **INT8 post-training quantisation**, judged specifically on **hand** agreement (the group with no
   headroom) and against the 5/5-identical-predictions bar RTMW FP16 set. 360 calibration frames from 6
   signers are already staged on the board.
5. **Apply the C9 protocol** to every row that will appear in the report, including the missing
   30-minute sustained run.
6. **Hands remain the accuracy floor and nothing addresses it.** Not precision-related, so INT8 will not
   fix it and may worsen it. Expect part of any BLEU spread across pose configs to be this noise.
