\begin{abstract}
We target continuous sign language translation on a 15\,W Jetson Orin Nano, where the
pipeline must consume one second of signed video in at most one second of wall clock. Our
baseline pairs an RTMW-l-m pose extractor with a Uni-Sign pose-only translator and reaches
a real-time factor of 1.064x at 23.16 BLEU-4 on 976 OpenASL clips, so it fails the latency
requirement. The pose stage is 6640.7 of the 9052.8\,ms per sentence and a cheaper
extractor costs 7.13 BLEU-4, so we propose vocabulary pruning of the translator, FP16
quantization of the extractor and frame-rate reduction to 24\,fps, each with a
re-measurement and a named failure mode.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
sign language translation, model compression, edge inference, Jetson Orin Nano
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Problem Statement}

The vision task is continuous sign language translation, which maps video frames of a
person signing to an English sentence, so the output is a sentence and not a sign label. A
whole-body pose extractor turns each frame into 133 keypoints, and a graph encoder
followed by an mT5 text decoder turns that keypoint sequence into English.

The deployment scenario is an assistive device on a counter or desk that translates
American Sign Language into English text during a face-to-face exchange between a Deaf
signer and a hearing person who does not sign. The input is video of
the signer, cropped to the signer bounding box at native resolution and at the clip's own
capture rate. The device must run on the board and not send frames to a server, because
these are private medical and transactional exchanges, and clinic desks and pharmacy
counters often have poor connectivity.

The constraint that binds is \textbf{latency}. The pipeline must consume one second of
signed video in at most one second of wall clock at 15\,W, which is a real-time factor of
at most 1.00x. The exchange is turn-taking, so above 1.00x the backlog grows with
every sentence and the English text arrives after the conversation has moved on. Our baseline measurement in Section~II gives
\textbf{1.064x}, so the baseline fails this constraint.

The accuracy floor is a \textbf{BLEU-4 loss of at most 1.00 point} against our baseline of
23.16, hence a floor of 22.16. We set it at 1.00 because the same checkpoint scores 22.67
in the paper, 22.53 replicating the authors' code on Colab in bf16, and 23.16 on our own
fp32 loop. That 0.6 spread comes from framework, precision and frame subsampling, not
run-to-run variance, so a tighter floor would reject a stage over a difference the
evaluation path produces by itself.

\section{Baseline Model}

The pose extractor is RTMW-l-m~\cite{rtmw} at 256$\times$192 producing 133 COCO-WholeBody keypoints, exported
to ONNX from the OpenMMLab deployment package~\cite{mmpose} and built as a TensorRT FP16 engine. The
translator is the released Uni-Sign pose-only checkpoint~\cite{unisign}, which pairs a
spatio-temporal graph encoder with mT5-base~\cite{mt5}, run in PyTorch at FP32. The
evaluation set is the OpenASL~\cite{openasl} test split, 976 clips, and the board clip is
a 255-frame, 8.51\,s sentence.

FP16 is the reference precision for the pose stage but not for the translator, where we
measured mT5 returning token~0 at every step at a log probability of $-10.169$, exactly
$-\ln(26078)$ and hence a uniform distribution from saturated logits.

Table~\ref{tab:baseline} reports our own measurements on course board
\texttt{jetson-lpcv-03} at 15\,W mode~0, GPU clock 612\,MHz, occupancy confirmed idle. The
pose row is 897 timed inferences (3 repeats of 299 frames) at batch~1. The sentence row is
3 repeats of one 8.51\,s clip, so its interval is run-to-run jitter and not variation
across sentences. Every stage below, and a re-measured baseline, is timed on 30 clips
spanning the duration range, 3 repeats each: 90 inferences rather than the protocol's
1000, which would hold a shared board for over two hours.

A smaller off-the-shelf model does not satisfy both requirements. The pose stage is 6640.7 of the 9052.8\,ms, so a cheaper extractor is the
obvious alternative and we tested it. Substituting RTMPose-x, the extractor the Uni-Sign
authors name, costs \textbf{7.13 BLEU-4}, 95\% CI $[-10.94, -3.07]$, because the
translator was trained on RTMW-family keypoints and another extractor shifts its input
distribution. That is seven times the accuracy floor, and within the family RTMW-l-m
256$\times$192 is already the smallest variant with a verified ONNX export. The
extractor is at its floor, so the remaining latency must come from running it fewer times
and from compressing the translator.

\section{Compression Plan}

We apply three stages with a measurement between each. We prune first because the
vocabulary slice halves peak memory, so later stages can be measured in the configuration
we deploy. We quantize second, and only the pose stage, because the
translator's numerics forbid it. We reduce the input rate last because its accuracy cost
must be charged against the already-compressed model. Distillation is excluded, as a
fourth stage would weaken the three.

\textbf{Stage 1: structured vocabulary pruning of the translator.} OpenASL uses 26{,}025
of the 250{,}112 mT5 tokens, so we slice those rows from the shared embedding and the
output projection and remove 344\,M of 587.7\,M parameters without retraining.
\emph{Re-measure:} BLEU-4 and ROUGE-L on 976 clips with a paired bootstrap, then board
latency, energy and peak memory. \emph{Failure mode A:} the keep set is selected using the
test split, which leaks evaluation data into the architecture, and we detect it by
re-running the census over train and dev only and re-scoring both keep sets.
\emph{Failure mode B:} a smaller softmax shifts beam scores even where the greedy argmax
is unchanged, so accuracy moves for a reason unrelated to capacity, and we count how many
of the 976 predictions change.

\textbf{Stage 2: FP16 quantization of the pose extractor.} We build the extractor as an
FP16 TensorRT engine~\cite{trt}. \emph{Re-measure:} keypoint agreement against the FP32 engine, as
the fraction of confident keypoints within 5\,px, then end-to-end BLEU-4. \emph{Failure
mode A:} one activation overflows the FP16 range and silently destroys the head. On
RTMPose-x we measured the ScaleNorm sum of squares at 142{,}293 vs the FP16 maximum of
65{,}504. We detect it before building, with a range scan that exposes every intermediate
tensor and runs the graph on ONNX Runtime CPU.
\emph{Failure mode B:} TensorRT enables TF32 by default, so an engine built as FP32 is not
FP32 and the gate uses the wrong reference. Clearing the TF32 builder flag moved our gate
from 1.55e-3 to 7.2e-6 with all 2416 confident keypoints at 0.0\,px. \emph{Failure mode C:} the
FP16 flag permits rather than forces, so the builder can keep layers above the requested
precision, and we report the fraction that actually run in FP16, read from the built
engine rather than from the build flag.

\textbf{Stage 3: input frame-rate reduction, 29.97 to 24\,fps.} The pose stage charges per
frame, so fewer frames is the largest single lever on the binding constraint.
\emph{Re-measure:} paired bootstrap on 976 clips at 30, 24, 16, 12 and 8\,fps, plus board
latency and energy over the rate by beam-width grid. \emph{Failure mode A:} the accuracy
cost is real but hidden by an interval too wide to exclude it. We made this error once,
reading a 16\,fps cost of $-0.15$, interval $[-3.20, +2.89]$ at $n{=}30$, as free, where
$n{=}976$ later gave $-1.33$, $[-2.00, -0.64]$. We fix $n{=}976$ in advance and refuse any
claim whose interval half-width exceeds the accuracy floor. \emph{Failure mode B:} OpenASL
is not one frame rate (76.5\% near 30, 22.2\% near 24), so a hardcoded source rate
subsamples the wrong frames, and we derive each clip's rate from frames divided by
duration.

Two techniques are excluded with a named reason. \emph{Weight-only INT8 on the translator}
is sound at 22.79 BLEU-4 and 4.05x smaller, but the decoder is bound by its host-side step
loop, so INT8 buys memory and not latency, and its runtime is 10x slower per token because
217 layers dequantize on every step. \emph{A TensorRT decoder engine set} is
token-identical at 19\,ms per token vs 55 for PyTorch, but is not faster end to end for the
same reason and needs 2.07\,GB resident, which does not fit beside the pose engine.

\textbf{Feasibility.} Fine-tuning is needed only if Stage~3 goes past 24\,fps. It runs on
Google Colab, to which we have access, and one epoch over 20{,}000 clips with mT5 frozen
trains 5.35\,M of 243.5\,M parameters in a few hours. We assume no desktop GPU and train
nothing on the board. We estimate 6 to 8 board-hours for the remaining measurements, and our 528-clip pose pass took 57.5 minutes. All three stages should complete by Milestone~2. The riskiest is
Stage~3 at 16\,fps, where the cost exceeds the floor and fine-tuning may not recover it,
hence the plan is 24\,fps, which needs none. Baseline and Stage~1--2 board measurements
are already taken.

\section{Metrics}

\label{m1}\textbf{Metrics.} The primary metric is the binding constraint of Section~I,
end-to-end sentence latency on the board at 15\,W, as P50 and P95 in ms and as a
real-time factor against the clip duration. Secondary metrics are energy per
sentence in J, total and dynamic, peak GPU memory in MiB, and per-stage energy integrated
from each stage's own power window rather than apportioned by latency share, since the
pose stage draws 5.0\,W and the language model 6.2\,W. Accuracy is BLEU-4 and ROUGE-L on
the 976-clip test split. Parameter counts and checkpoint sizes are context, not results.

\label{m2}\textbf{Baseline.} Every comparison is against the Table~\ref{tab:baseline}
configuration, in the same runtime, on the same board and the same 30 clips, and for
accuracy on the same 976 clips.

\label{m3}\textbf{Decision rule.} The project succeeds if the compressed pipeline reaches
a real-time factor of at most 1.00x at 15\,W \emph{and} the BLEU-4 loss against baseline
has a 95\% confidence interval whose lower bound is above $-1.00$ point. It fails otherwise. The rule is stated on the interval and not on the point estimate, so a result
cannot pass on a loss that the data cannot actually bound.

\label{evidencestart}\textbf{Evidence.} Four ablations isolate each stage: vocabulary
pruning on and off, frame rate at 30, 24, 16, 12 and 8\,fps, beam width at 4, 2 and 1, and
pose FP16 vs FP32. Every board row is 3 repeats with the DVFS state logged, and every accuracy difference is a
paired clip-level bootstrap on one shared set of draws.
